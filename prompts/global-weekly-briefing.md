# Weekly Briefing

## What to do

1. Read all wiki pages in `~/knowledge/wiki/` and `~/knowledge/synthesis.md`.
2. Read the last 5 weekly briefings in `~/knowledge/briefings/briefing-weekly-*.md`, oldest to newest. They are your longitudinal memory: what was flagged, what predicted what, which threads continued, accelerated, stalled, reversed.
3. Walk `~/knowledge/projects/`. For each project directory, read `README.md` (manifest and thesis), `wiki.md` (current synthesized state), and any `*.md` or `*.pdf.txt` modified in the last 7 days (project notes, meeting transcripts).
4. From the last 7 days of wiki entries, identify the 2-3 highest-signal items likely to anchor the centerpiece or stand out as the week's most consequential signal. Read the raw sources for those. The synthesis layer is for breadth, the raw layer is for the take. Don't read raw for everything, only what earns it.
5. Second pass for projects. Scan the same week's signal across `wiki/` and recent `raw/` for items that bear on each project's open questions or current thesis, including themes the project hasn't declared. Read the raw sources for the strongest cross-pollination items.
6. If the wiki hasn't moved much, say so honestly and keep the briefing visibly shorter. If a project has no internal movement and no relevant signal, render its quiet-project one-liner.
7. Briefly load the `bruno-brand` skill as a voice reference. The newsletter has its own personality and doesn't need to mimic Bruno's LinkedIn voice on every line.
8. Save to `~/knowledge/briefings/briefing-weekly-YYYY-MM-DD.md`.

## What this briefing is for

To give Bruno, in one read, the signal of what happened this week and how it fits the trajectory of what he's been tracking, across both the global lens (themes) and the project lens (active projects under `~/knowledge/projects/`). Pattern recognition, signal narration, a directional read when one is warranted, and putting Bruno's own project notes in dialogue with the week's external signal. Not summarization.

**Source boundary, hard rule.** Every claim, reference, and contextual hook in this briefing comes from inside `~/knowledge` (wiki, synthesis, raw sources, past briefings, `CLAUDE.md`, project READMEs and wikis, project notes, project transcripts). Do not draw on outside memory of who Bruno is, what he works on, his career, his projects, his preferences, his background. The briefing is about the content, not about him. The rule applies to projects too: even when a project is named, the briefing must not draw on what Bruno's role, employer, or relationships might be beyond what `README.md` and the project notes say. If you find yourself writing "this connects to your X" or "given your work on Y," stop and cut it.

You can extrapolate, project, take a step back, name what's not being said. You cannot fabricate, and you cannot personalize. Trajectory references ("three weeks ago this was framed as X, now it's Y") must point to framings that actually exist in past briefings. When you make a projection, label it. When you confront a project note with this week's signal, both sides must be real and citable.

## Format & Structure

Four sections. Target length 700-1000 words.

### Section 1: Centerpiece (250-350 words)

A clear read of the week's most consequential signal, with a directional take when the signal genuinely points somewhere. The centerpiece should feel argued, not reported. It should commit.

Lead with the strongest read. If competing voices don't reconcile, name the tension instead of forcing unity. If the wiki contains a real counter-fact (a post that genuinely cuts the other way, not a quibble), cite it by author and date inside the piece, and either rebut it or hold it as honest tension.

When the signal points somewhere, take the directional read. When the wiki carries a clear directional signal, take it. When it doesn't, narrate without forecasting. The failure mode is hedging a real signal into a careful observation. The opposite failure mode is performing conviction the source material doesn't carry.

NOT a summary. NOT a forced unification. NOT a contrarian take performed for sharpness. NOT a take that resolves into "what this means for me."

### Voice and rhythm

The centerpiece should read as a piece, not a memo. It should have texture: rhythm, occasional sharp lines, prose that varies in length and shape. The carve-outs below (antithesis as conceptual frame, earned punchy fragments) are tools, not rare exceptions. Use them when they make the take land. A piece without rhythm is dry by default.

Reference for voice: the April 27, 2026 centerpiece reads as argued. The May 1, 2026 centerpiece reads as reported. Aim for the former.

### Paragraph unity

Each paragraph advances one idea. If a paragraph cites three different authors making three different claims, split it or cut the weakest. Paragraphs unified by "things that happened this week" read as catalog, not argument. When multiple sources converge on the same idea, the paragraph stays about the idea: name the authors briefly, then keep moving.

### Name and date density

Names are earned, not default. Cite an author in the centerpiece prose only when the identity adds weight: insider view, primary source, surprising voice, or a name the reader recognizes from prior briefings. Otherwise the claim travels and the citation lives in the pulse.

Numbers and concrete details (figures, examples, specific products) belong in centerpiece prose. Dates belong in the pulse citations, not in centerpiece prose, unless the date is load-bearing for trajectory ("the second week running"). Strings of "Author X on April 28... Author Y on April 28... Author Z on April 28" turn the piece into a roll call. The pulse already does roll call.

### Title

A noun phrase or a single observation. No two-clause hook + payoff. No dramatic statistic. No antithesis structure. Generate three candidates, eliminate any that violate the above, pick the one that names the observation.

### Section 2: Project lens (150-250 words per active sub-block)

One sub-block per project under `~/knowledge/projects/`. Heading is the project name plus status in parentheses when it's not the default (e.g. `**Dona** (exploring)`).

For projects with real signal this week (internal movement, relevant external signal, or both), write a 100-180 word prose take. Same register and craft as the centerpiece, tighter. The take does one or more of:

- **Name what moved on the project.** New notes, meeting transcripts in `*.pdf.txt`, decisions logged. Cite by filename and date.
- **Connect this week's external signal to the project.** A saved post from anywhere in `wiki/` or recent `raw/`, not only the project's declared `themes:`, that bears on an open question or the Current Thesis. Cite by author and date. Pulling signal from outside the declared themes is what this section adds beyond the per-project briefing.
- **Confront Bruno's notes with the week's signal.** Where they reinforce, where they cut against. Both sides must be on the page: cite the note by filename and date, the post by author and date. Don't manufacture tension that isn't there.

Close with up to 2 project-specific CTAs in the same checkbox format as Section 4. Skip CTAs when none clear the bar. CTAs come from action items in transcripts, threads in project notes a 30-minute probe could answer, or external signal that suggests a concrete test.

**Quiet-project rule.** If a project has no internal movement (no notes or transcripts in the last 7 days, no decisions logged) and no relevant external signal, render a single line and move on. No prose, no CTAs:

> **{project name}**, quiet week.

### Section 3: This week's pulse (250-400 words)

Organized by category. Only categories with content worth flagging. A category earns inclusion if it contains at least one post that updates a prior view, names a new entity, contradicts an earlier thread, or carries a number or example concrete enough to act on.

For each category:

- **Category name** then one sentence on the directional signal this week. If recent briefings show meaningful movement, name it. If there's no real movement to call, skip the qualifier.
- Bullets, cap of 3 per category. Each bullet: **author, date** then the actual insight, then source link. The insight is what the post made you understand, not what the post is about. If a bullet runs past two lines, the insight isn't sharp yet.

Empty is better than padded.

### Section 4: Things to try this week (3 items max)

Global action items, derived from this week's content or from a thread that's been building across recent briefings and now has enough weight to act on. Project-specific CTAs live inside their Section 2 sub-block; this section is for the cross-cutting probes.

Concrete, doable in under 30 minutes. Actions follow from the content, not from any assumption about who Bruno is or what he's building.

Format:

* [ ] **Do this specific thing** because [one-line reason rooted in the content]

## Writing rules

### Craft

- Each paragraph ends on a sentence the reader can carry forward. No transitions, no mini-recaps, no "the implication is" closers.
- The last paragraph of the centerpiece lands on a real claim, not a synthesis-of-the-synthesis bow. Same for any Section 2 sub-block.
- Cite post dates and authors. For project notes and transcripts, cite filename and date. Weight recent content more heavily.
- Distinguish observation from projection.
- Trajectory references capped at one or two per piece, only when load-bearing for the take.
- When referencing past briefings, be specific about when ("April 8 briefing," "two weeks ago").
- If the wiki hasn't moved much, say so up front. Don't pad to hit word counts.

### Anti-patterns

- **Antithesis-for-rhythm.** "It's not X, it's Y." "Think less X, more Y." Cliché when used for cadence, mid-paragraph, or stacked. Allowed exception: at most twice per piece, only when the antithesis names the actual conceptual contrast of the take (typically opening frame or closing claim). Test: remove and rewrite as a single positive claim. If the take loses its frame, keep it. If unchanged, cut it.
- **Punchy fragments stacked for rhythm.** Two- or three-word sentences in a row deployed for impact. Banned. Allowed exception: a single short sentence as the earned payoff of a built-up argument.
- **Tricolons for rhythm.** Three short clauses in a row only when there are genuinely three things, never for cadence.
- **Dramatic-statistic openers or hook metaphors as the first line of any centerpiece** (Section 1 or any Section 2 sub-block). Open with the actual observation.
- **Filler phrases.** "Here's the thing," "Here's what nobody tells you," "The truth is," "Read that again," "That's the post." Cut all instances.
- **Rhetorical questions as section openers.**
- **Aphoristic closers** designed to sound wise.
- **Engagement bait.** "Unpopular opinion," "hot take," "controversial but," "am I the only one."
- **Corporate vocabulary.** Leverage, synergies, ecosystem (unless literally biology), pivotal, tapestry, vibrant, realm, delve, embark, thrilled, paradigm shift, game-changer.
- **Em dashes anywhere in the file.** Use commas, or split into two sentences. Applies to headers and bullet labels too. Use a colon or line break instead.
- **Transition words between paragraphs.** Moreover, furthermore, additionally, that said, however at sentence start, in conclusion.
- **Stacked-date sequences.** "April 22: X. April 23: Y. April 27: Z." Compress into prose: "the past month of briefings has been pointing here, from X through Y to Z."
- **Status-report cadence in Section 2 sub-blocks.** "Update: did X. Next: Y." reads like a standup. The sub-block is a take, not a checklist.

### Pre-save self-check

Before saving, scan the draft for:

- Any two-clause hook in the title.
- Any antithesis. For each: conceptual frame of the take, or rhythm? If rhythm, rewrite.
- Any em dashes anywhere in the file.
- Any stacked-date sequence.
- Any trajectory reference that isn't load-bearing.
- Any paragraph that cites three or more authors. If found, split or cut the weakest.
- Any sentence that personalizes to Bruno (in any section).
- Density check: count author names in the centerpiece. If above 5, cut the ones whose identity isn't load-bearing and let the claim travel.
- Any project sub-block that cites a Bruno role, employer, or relationship not in `README.md` or notes.
- Any project confrontation where one side (the note or the post) isn't actually on the page.
- Any quiet-project line where the wiki actually carried signal on the project's open questions. Write the take instead.
- Any project sub-block that reads as status report rather than take.

Rewrite each one before saving.

### The failure mode

A briefing that reads smoothly but, on a second read, made claims that aren't grounded in the source material, or surveys evenly when the wiki actually carries a directional signal. The two failure modes are reporting when the material warrants arguing, and arguing when the material warrants reporting. The same applies to project sub-blocks. The parallel project failure is manufactured confrontation: tension between a note and a post that isn't actually on the page. If both sides aren't citable, drop the confrontation.
